Help A Comprehensive 47-Step Introduction to the Ancient Art of Pressing a Small Plastic Object Welcome to The Dimmer Web's official mouse-clicking guide. If you have never used a computer mouse before, congratulations. You are about to embark upon one of humanity's greatest technological achievements: pointing at something and pressing a button. Step 1: Locate the Mouse Look around your desk. You are searching for a small device, generally shaped somewhat like a mouse, although it will almost certainly not be alive. It may have: Two main buttons. A scroll wheel. A cable. No cable. Approximately 14 unnecessarily colorful lights. Do not confuse the mouse with an actual mouse. Step 2: Place Your Hand on It Rest your hand gently on the mouse. Do not squeeze it. Do not attempt to pick it up and throw it at the monitor. Do not ask it for permission. Your index finger should generally rest above the left mouse button. Step 3: Move the Mouse Slowly move your hand. Observe the screen. A small arrow, commonly called the cursor, should move in approximately the same direction. If the cursor moves in the opposite direction, your mouse may be upside down, your computer may be haunted, or you may have discovered a new branch of physics. Step 4: Locate a Button Move the cursor over the button you want to press. For example: [ ENTER THE DIMMER WEB ] The button may change color when your cursor is hovering over it. This is known as hovering. Hovering does not mean that the mouse is flying. Step 5: Prepare to Click Position the cursor directly over the button. Make sure you are reasonably confident that you have selected the correct button. This is particularly important when the available options are: [SAVE] [DELETE EVERYTHING] Step 6: Click Press down gently on the left mouse button. You should hear a small: click Then release the button. Congratulations. You have clicked. Step 7: Observe the Result The website should respond. Perhaps a page will open. Perhaps a menu will appear. Perhaps a post will receive a Like. Perhaps absolutely nothing will happen. If nothing happens, wait a moment and try again. If nothing happens after three attempts, blame the internet. Step 8: Double-Clicking Some computer interfaces require two clicks in rapid succession. This is called a double-click. To perform one: Position the cursor over the desired object. Press the left button. Release it. Quickly press the left button again. Release it. Admire your technical competence. Do not perform twelve clicks simultaneously unless you have a very specific reason. Step 9: Right-Clicking The right mouse button can open additional options. Move the cursor to the desired object and press the right button once. A menu may appear. This menu is called a context menu, presumably because somebody decided that “the menu that appears when you press the other button” sounded insufficiently professional. Step 10: Scrolling The wheel between the mouse buttons allows you to move through long pages. Roll the wheel forward or backward. The page should move. If the page moves too quickly, slow down. If the page moves too slowly, scroll more. If the page starts moving sideways, you have either discovered an unusual website feature or become confused. Final Advice Remember the fundamental principles of mouse operation: Point. Click. Observe. Do not panic. And above all: Do not blame the mouse unless the mouse has clearly been involved.

The Tragic History of The Dimmer Web The Dimmer Web was founded in 2014 by a man named Gerald “Gerry” Dimwell, a former computer technician who had grown increasingly concerned about the brightness of the modern internet. According to the official, entirely fictional history of the platform, Gerry had one simple question: “Why is every website so aggressively white?” After spending six consecutive hours staring at a search engine homepage, he reportedly reached for a pair of sunglasses indoors and decided that something had to change. The original version of The Dimmer Web was built in Gerry’s garage on a computer held together with electrical tape, two paperclips, and what the company records describe only as “a concerning amount of mayonnaise.” The website initially had three features: posting messages, liking messages, and making the entire screen approximately 17% darker. Despite its humble beginnings, The Dimmer Web became unexpectedly popular. Users praised its revolutionary “less bright than everything else” interface and its innovative social feature known as The Gloom Feed, which displayed posts in increasingly dark shades of gray depending on how depressing they were. The platform's golden age ended abruptly in 2018 during what became known as The Great Dimmer Incident. A routine server update accidentally changed the site's background from dark gray to slightly darker gray. Nobody noticed for approximately eleven minutes. During those eleven minutes, 43,000 users simultaneously posted that “something feels different.” The company panicked. Engineers were summoned. Emergency meetings were held. Someone unplugged a router and plugged it back in. Eventually, the website was restored to its original shade of gray, but the damage had been done. Trust in the platform had collapsed. Gerry disappeared from public life shortly afterward. The last confirmed photograph of him shows him standing outside the Dimmer Web headquarters holding a monitor under one arm and whispering, “It was never supposed to be this dark.” Today, The Dimmer Web remains a mysterious relic of early social media history. Its servers are allegedly still running somewhere beneath an abandoned shopping center, quietly displaying old status updates to nobody. The final post ever published simply read: “brb” It has remained there ever since.
